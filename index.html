Guidance

How to choose colours, how to put them together and how to use a combination, from the advice of Wada Sanzō, who made the combinations on this site, and of Hama Yaohiko, who made a colour atlas with him. Advice not marked otherwise is Wada's, and notes marked Since then add what colour research has found since.

Wada didn't say which of his combinations show which point. The examples here are chosen for this site by their colours' lightness, saturation and hue, and each opens its page. The ideas behind the advice are explained in Colour theory.

How Wada saw it

  • What the combinations are for. Wada made his book for everyone, not only for artists. By then everyone chose the colours of their own clothes and rooms, yet books on combining colours were written for specialists, and gave colours as names, which can't pin a colour down. So he showed the colours themselves, as "a handy colour adviser for everyday use", for art and crafts, dress, architecture and interiors, furniture, shop windows and advertising.

    He didn't think the book complete. Tastes keep changing, and he meant to improve it with readers' help.

  • Taste, not a formula. Combining colours can't be taught like mathematics, where a formula gives the right answer. Each person judges by their own feeling, and taste in colour is like a singing voice, partly a gift but improved by practice. So Wada gave rules of thumb from a painter's experience, not laws. Hama didn't set laws either, since what people like depends on custom, age, upbringing and surroundings.

    Since then: tests bear this out for liking, but find other judgements widely shared. In eight countries, people judged pairs of colours much alike as warm or cool, heavy or light, and lively or calm, but whether they liked a pair varied with culture, sex, age and training in design, much as Hama said. In tests in the United States, most people also found pairs of similar hue more harmonious than pairs of very different hue. No one has found a formula.

  • Taste changes. It differs from nation to nation. Wada found the shop windows of Paris more refined than those of Berlin or London, and put it down to a taste the whole nation shared. It changes over time too. Under Louis XIV, French silks had large, rich patterns, and less than a century later, under Louis XVI, they were cream or pale pink with small flowers. Japan's textiles from the Heian to the Edo period show the same.

    So old rules go out of date. By the rules of the French chemist Michel-Eugène Chevreul, red with blue is pleasing, yet few in Wada's day agreed, and brown beside black, long frowned on, had become well liked.

    Since then: one explanation of why taste differs, from tests in the United States, is that people like colours they link with things they like, such as the blue of clear skies and clean water, and dislike those they link with things they dislike, such as the browns of rotten food. Students at Berkeley and Stanford each liked their own university's colours more than their rival's, the more so the more school spirit they had.

Choosing a colour

Wada and Hama describe how colours feel, but seldom how to get the feeling you want. So the advice here comes from colour research since their time, with their own points added, and marked as theirs, where they fit.

  • Start from the feeling. Decide what the colour should do, whether it should look warm or cool, lively or calm, light or heavy. Each feeling depends mostly on one of a colour's three attributes, so you can set them one at a time. In tests in Britain and China, warm or cool depended mostly on hue, lively or calm on saturation, and light or heavy on lightness.

  • Warmer or cooler. Move the hue. The warmest colours are orange-reds and the coolest blues, so a red leaning towards orange is warm and one leaning towards purple cooler. The stronger the colour, the stronger the feeling, and lightness makes little difference. Wada's own warmer and cooler greens and yellows lean the same way (see Strong and weak, warm and cool).

    Warmth is also relative. Beside an orange a red looks cooler, and beside a purple warmer (see Contrast).

  • Livelier or calmer. Change the saturation. A strong colour looks lively, as Wada also found, and a muted one of middle lightness looks calmest.

    Most colours in use are muted. Sorting tens of thousands of samples for Wada's standard colours of 1930, the technician at his studio found few pure colours among them.

  • Lighter or heavier. Change the lightness. Dark colours look heavy, and pale ones light, almost whatever their hue. Wada found a combination of dark colours alone suffocating (see What makes colours go together).

  • Readable from afar. Text and signs read best when they differ strongly in lightness from their ground. A difference in hue adds little, so a red on a green just as light is hard to read, however opposite the two hues (see Contrast).

    Hama's commentary gives two tests. In the first, black on yellow could be read from furthest away, then green, red and blue on white, and red on green came last. In the second, with small cards seen from about 22 metres, white stood out best on green, red and blue grounds, and black on white. Lightness contrast, Hama concluded, decided the order.

    OKLCH gives lightness as L, its first number, from 0 for black to 1 for white. The further apart the L of text and ground, the better the text reads. Guidelines for text on screens, such as WCAG, also go by lightness alone, measured as luminance. They ask for a contrast of at least 4.5 to 1 for ordinary text, which means L values roughly 0.4 apart, or up to about 0.55 when the darker colour is near black.

  • Judge it where it will be used. A colour changes with the light, the material and its size (see What changes a colour), so look at a sample in the place and the light it's for.

    • Material. The same dye comes out differently on silk, cotton and hemp (Wada), so judge a colour on the material it will be made in.
    • Size. Colours look paler and weaker on a large object than in a small swatch (Wada). Measurements since find large areas lighter, but more vivid, so a wall may need a paint a step greyer than its sample.

  • Lasting colour. Colours fade, some far sooner than others. Choose paints and inks by their lightfastness rating, and keep colours out of strong sun (see What changes a colour). Even Wada's cards, painted only in pigments that had passed a fading test, were to be kept out of direct sunlight, in a dry, dark place.

What makes colours go together

  • Balance lightness and saturation, not only hue. Lining up well-chosen hues isn't enough. A combination works through how its colours' lightness and saturation balance one another.

    • Vivid colours together need a muted one among them or beside them.
    • Pale colours together feel lukewarm, and need a deep one, in a measured amount.
    • Dark colours together are suffocating, and some of them must be lighter.

    It's like cooking, where a meal comes together through the balance of hot, sweet and sour.

    • Vivid Benzol Green and Jasper Red, with a muted Deep Indigo.

    • Three pale colours, with a deep Brick Red.

    • Two dark colours, Dull Violet Black and Brown, with a lighter Citron Yellow.

  • Equal strength is safe, but ordinary. When every colour is about as strong as the others, none sets off another. The result is safe but commonplace, the kind anyone makes.

    • Three pale colours, about as strong as one another.

    For something livelier, make some colours stronger than others, so that they set one another off.

    • Two pale colours, Seashell Pink and Glaucous Green, set off by a stronger Citron Yellow.

  • Tone (Hama). Colours of one tone, about equally light and strong, sit together. Colours of different tones, used well, add life and save a design from flatness, but used thoughtlessly they're off-key, as in music.

    • One tone, Eosine Pink, Orange Yellow and Citron Yellow, about equally light and strong.

    • Two unrelated tones, a pale Hermosa Pink and a vivid Yellow, close to the baby pink and bright yellow that a design guide lists as a clash.

  • Variety and unity (Hama). Opposite colours give variety and look beautiful at first, but they tire with long looking, and people come to prefer the milder combinations they first thought dull. So balance variety and unity, for the purpose in hand.

    • Clear opposites, a strong red, Eugenia Red, and a blue-green about as light, Sea Green.

    • Where there is too much variety, change some colours' lightness and saturation.

      • The same opposite hues, the blue-green now pale and greyish, Nile Blue beside Etruscan Red.

    • Where there are many colours, let one of them dominate, to hold the rest together.

      • Five colours, from a strong Apricot Yellow to a deep Violet Blue, held together by a large, soft Grayish Lavender.

      • A blue, a green, a red, a yellow and an olive, held together by a large Olive Ocher.

Kinds of harmony

In his 1935 supplement, Wada sorts harmony into four kinds.

Like colours

One hue in its light and dark tones, such as orange with its browns and its pale, flesh-like tints. Hama adds neighbouring hues, such as orange with a yellower or a redder orange, or blue with a purplish blue, sometimes with one of them made stronger or weaker.

It's the most common kind, and gentle, because the steps from tone to tone have no sudden jumps. Whoever handles it, there is no fear of a great failure, and it's widely liked as mild and pleasant (Hama). But it seldom gives more than ordinary harmony.

So it's the place for a beginner to start, rather than going straight for different hues and failing. To lift it above the ordinary, let its colours differ in strength, so that they set one another off (see What makes colours go together).

  • Close to Wada's own example, an orange's pale, flesh-like tint, Seashell Pink, with one of its browns, Vandyke Brown.

  • One red, strong and pale, Pompeian Red and Cameo Pink.

  • Neighbouring hues, Pale Lemon Yellow and a stronger Yellow Green.

Graded bands

One hue in steps, called ungen (暈繝), as in the banded colouring of temple ornament. Wada describes it with blue. Put the full blue in the middle, then step lighter and lighter to one side, ending in white, and darker and darker to the other, ending in black.

  • His blue scale, in the book's own blues, from White through Salvia Blue, Olympic Blue, the full Blue, Deep Lyons Blue and Deep Indigo to Black.

A combination takes part of that scale, and it's calmer still than like colours. Choose its colours at even steps along the scale, every second or every third (Hama).

Ostwald's blue page shows such scales, with Wada's colours marked. From the full blue at its right tip, the tints run up to white and the shades down to black, and the greyed blues fill the space between.

  • One red in three steps, from the dark Vistoris Lake through Spinel Red to the pale Cameo Pink.

The steps can also move gradually round the hue circle, from one colour into its neighbours (Hama).

  • The colours of combination 281 in step order, from blue to yellow, each a step further round and lighter, Antwerp Blue, Benzol Green, Cobalt Green and Pale Lemon Yellow.

Different hues

Colours of quite different hues, light or dark alike, such as a red with an orange, a yellow, a green, a blue or a purple. The freest kind, and more interesting than like colours, but harder to get right.

Where the hues touch they oppose each other, so weigh each colour's lightness and strength, or the result turns coarse.

  • A blue, a purple and an orange, each at its own lightness, Antwerp Blue, Red Violet and the lighter Cinnamon Rufous.

  • Balance lightness and saturation. Choosing the hues well isn't enough. Among vivid colours put a muted one, and among pale ones a deep one (see What makes colours go together).

  • Two primaries (Chevreul). Two of red, yellow and blue go together better than one of them with a colour mixed from it, such as red with orange or purple. Such a pair works best when the primary is the brighter.

  • Three or more colours (Hama). Choose hues that divide the hue circle about equally, such as red, yellow and blue, or red, green and blue, with one or two of them made lighter or darker.

    • A blue, a yellow and a purplish red, about a third of the circle apart, the blue dark and the yellow light.

  • Where two clash, put white, grey or black between them (see White, grey or black between colours).

Complementary

Opposite hues, whatever their lightness. Really a special case of different hues, but stronger than the others, so when to use them is debated.

Each sets the other off (see Complementary colours), and Chevreul counts them good harmony by contrast. Used well they give lively, excellent combinations, though for fewer uses. They tire with long looking, though, so where a pair is too lively, change one colour's lightness and saturation (see Variety and unity).

  • Deep Lyons Blue and Yellow, one of the pairs in his own table (below).

  • Calmer, Yellow Orange, which his table pairs with the vivid Antwerp Blue, here with a darker, greyer blue, Dark Tyrian Blue.

Wada explains complements by after-images. Stare at a red paper, take it away, and green appears in its place. Yellow leaves ultramarine, and purple a green-yellow.

He drew up a table of complementary pairs from his own palette. He paired them by hue alone, so their lightness and saturation vary. A colour's lighter and darker tones pair too. and go with , as does.

In tests that averaged many people's judgements, cited by Hama, complementary pairs, or pairs a little off them, were good, and a colour with the hue halfway to its complement was poor.

  • Good. Red and blue, red and blue-green, orange and green-blue (or blue), yellow and violet, yellow and blue, yellow-green and violet, green and red-purple. Wada had his doubts about red with blue (see Taste changes).
  • Poor. Red and orange, red and yellow, orange and yellow, yellow and green (or blue-green), yellow-green and orange (or green-blue), green and blue.

Since then: recent tests disagree. Pairs of very different hue are usually judged neither harmonious nor pleasing, and pairs of similar hue more of both. But people liked a small colour on a ground better the more its hue differed from the ground's, so contrasting hues suit a figure set on a background.

Ten kinds, in 1934

His earlier booklet, of 1934, sorts harmony another way, into ten kinds, two kinds in five tones each.

  • One family. Colours of one family, changing gradually, which he likens to modulation in music. A family means neighbouring colours, not one colour. His example is a kimono outfit, a sky-blue haori, a kimono with a touch of ultramarine, an obi greener than sky blue and a yellowish-green collar.

    • His outfit in colours from the book, our choice, a Salvia Blue haori (the book's sky blue), a bluer Olympic Blue kimono, a greener Calamine Blue obi and a Yellow Green collar.

  • Wholly unlike. Colours of entirely different quality. The hardest kind, but the most rewarding.

    • Orange, Dark Tyrian Blue and Yellow Green.

The five tones are these.

  • Light. Bright colours, equally strong.

    • One family, light, Yellow Orange, Yellow Ocher and Orange Rufous, about equally bright and strong.

  • Dark. Dark colours, yet somewhat alike in tone. His 1935 advice adds that some must be made lighter, since dark colours alone are suffocating (see What makes colours go together).

    • Dark purples, Cotinga Purple and Violet, with a somewhat lighter Carmine Red.

  • Pale. Neighbouring colours, alike in tone, their strength weakened. His 1935 advice adds a deep colour, in a measured amount, since pale colours alone feel lukewarm.

    • Olive Buff and Pinkish Cinnamon, pale and alike in tone, with a deep Blue Violet.

  • Deep. Rich, deep colours fused together, strongly stimulating.

    • Wholly unlike and deep, Red Violet, Scarlet and Dull Viridian Green.

  • Free (pan, from the Greek). Tones passing freely into one another, mixing light and dark, pale and deep at will.

    • One family in free tones, a strong Lemon Yellow, a pale Cinnamon Buff and a dark Lincoln Green.

For clothes, interiors and furniture, the safe choice is one family in free tones, which anyone can manage.

He meant the kinds as a standard, not rules to obey. Those with long experience of colour can merge them or break them.

Using a combination

  • Choose for the purpose and place. Strong combinations catch the eye, which suits advertising, shop windows and the stage, but many find them vulgar, and they tire quickly. For things you live with, choose calm ones. Calm doesn't mean dull, but neighbouring hues, their strengths well balanced.

    • Strong, Carmine, Yellow and Blue, each near full strength.

    • Calm but not dull, three neighbouring yellows, Citrine, Golden Yellow and Sulphur Yellow.

  • Give the colours different areas. The book shows every colour the same size only for convenience. Equal areas balance only when the colours are equally strong, so give the weak tones the large areas and the strong ones the small.

    • As the book shows it, a weak Cameo Pink and a strong Pompeian Red at equal areas.

    • The same two, the weak Cameo Pink large and the strong Pompeian Red small.

    • On one of his cards, a weak Light Glaucous Blue on the large area, a strong Red on a small one.

  • Find the main colour. On the seasonal cards, the largest colour, on top, is the main one, holding the rest together.

    In actual dress a small one often takes that role, such as the obi, the collar, a hat, gloves or a handbag. Where is the centre of the combination? That is the question (see his second card).

  • Try the amounts. Lay cut white or grey paper over a combination, to change each colour's shape and size. The book came with a sheet of cut-out papers of several shapes for this. Wada sets no ratio, so the amounts are yours to try.

    For a kimono, lay a triangle over three colours, so that its tip gives the collar, the middle colour the kimono and the last the obi. For more colours, cut papers of your own, and count the white of the mount as one more colour.

    • Three colours at equal areas, as the book shows them.

    • The same in kimono amounts, a sliver of Neutral Gray for the collar, Deep Slate Olive for the kimono and Golden Yellow for the obi.

    On this site, Create does the same with its switches. Main draws one colour large and the others as small bands, as on the seasonal cards, and Auto gives the large area to the weakest colour.

  • Allow for size. A combination that works in small chips looks paler and weaker on a large object (Wada), so judge it at the size it will be used (see Choosing a colour).

  • Glossy materials help (Chevreul). Colours go together better on glossy materials, such as silk or glass, than on matt ones, such as cotton or wool. Two bright colours, too, go together better than duller ones.

Making your own

Wada also meant his readers to make combinations of their own. His seasonal cards were movable, opening like a fan, so that anyone could try combinations besides his. On this site, Create lays out your own as in the book, or in other shapes, with one colour large if you like.

Put together, his advice gives these steps.

  • Start from one hue, in its light and dark tones (see Like colours and Graded bands). It can hardly go wrong.

    • One blue, the dark Deep Indigo and the light Salvia Blue, with Neutral Gray.

  • Balance lightness and saturation. Among vivid colours put a muted one, among pale ones a deep one, and among dark ones some lighter (see What makes colours go together).

  • Let the strengths differ. When every colour is as strong as the others, the result is safe but ordinary, as on his first card.

  • Then try different hues, with care. Where they touch, weigh each colour's lightness and strength (see Different hues and Complementary).

  • Where two clash, put white, grey or black between them (see below).

  • Then the amounts. Decide which colour leads, and give the weak tones the large areas (see Using a combination).

White, grey or black between colours

Where two colours clash side by side, put white, grey or black between them, minding each colour's saturation and lightness. Which to use follows the rules of Chevreul, to Wada the leading authority on colour contrast and harmony.

Which one depends on the two colours.

  • Red and yellow. White or grey, not black.

    • Grey between them in the book, Carmine Red and Pale Lemon Yellow.

  • Red and blue. Grey or black, not white.

    • Black between Spectrum Red and Blue.

  • Blue and yellow. White or black, not grey.

    • White between Blue and Yellow.

  • Yellow and orange. White or grey, not black.

    • Neutral Gray between Yellow and Orange.

  • Blue and purple. White or black, not grey.

    • White between Blue and Violet.

  • Orange and green. Black.

    • Black between Orange and Green.

  • Purples. Grey.

    • Neutral Gray between Violet and Red Violet.

  • Purple and green. White.

    • White between Violet and Green.

Black and grey each suit some colours more than others.

  • Black never clashes with two bright colours, and mostly suits dark purples and blues. With one bright colour and one dark, it pleases less.

    • Black between two bright colours, Orange and Rosolane Purple.

  • Grey suits two bright colours, and never clashes even with two dull ones. With dull, dark blues or purples, black is better. With a bright colour and a dark one, grey is better than white, and over large areas black also works.

    • Neutral Gray between a bright Orange and a dark Deep Indigo.

The same goes for outlines in a design.

  • On a pale ground, draw patterns in deep tones, outlined in black, grey or a colour deeper still.

    • A deep red pattern, Carmine Red, on a pale ground, outlined in black.

  • A pattern paler than its ground takes a white or grey outline.

    • A pale blue pattern on a deep ground, outlined in white.

  • A pattern in light and dark steps of one colour takes a white or grey outline on a deep ground, and a deep one on a pale ground.

    • Steps of one blue on a deep ground, outlined in white.

    • The same steps on a pale ground, outlined in a deep blue.

By Wada's own account, though, taste has moved on since Chevreul, so not every rule still holds (see Taste changes).

Since then: two colours that meet with nothing between them change each other by contrast along the edge, and a line or gap between them stops it, which may be one reason this works (see Contrast).

Colour theory

Before combining colours, it helps to know what a colour is made of. This page is a short introduction to colour theory: what colour is, how to describe it, how colours mix and act on one another, and how they are ordered and named.

The aim is to help you understand Wada Sanzō's work: the basics of colour as he understood them, from the booklets that went with his combinations (1934 and 1935), and the system and names he wrote his colours in. Hama Yaohiko, who made a colour atlas with him in 1935, covered the same ground in more detail in its commentary, and fills in much of what follows. Where he saw something differently, the text says so. Colour science has moved on since the 1930s, so where it now says otherwise, a note explains what changed. The site also adds three other colour systems to Ostwald's.

You don't need any of it to use the combinations, but it explains the words Guidance uses and how Wada colours and Colour systems order the palette.

Light and colour

Colour needs light. In the dark, nothing has a colour. White light, such as sunlight, is a mix of all the colours, and a prism spreads it out into the spectrum, from red to violet.

An object absorbs part of the light that falls on it and reflects the rest, and the light it reflects is what we see as its colour. A pigment absorbs about half the spectrum and reflects the half around its own colour. A red that reflected only red light would look almost black, not bright red, because it would reflect so little light.

An object that absorbed all light would look black, and one that reflected all of it would look white. White, black and the greys between them are colours without hue. No material is perfectly white or perfectly black, so whites differ from one another, and so do blacks, just as other colours do.

The light itself isn't coloured, though. The colour is made in the eye and the brain. In the eye, cones see colour in daylight, and rods, which see only light and dark, take over in dim light.

So a colour is never quite fixed. It changes with the light, with the colours around it and with the material it's on. That is true of this site too, as Wada's colours are printed inks, which reflect light, while a screen gives off light of its own. The colours here are computed from the books' printing values. They come close to the printed page, but every screen shows them a little differently.

Describing a colour

Wada and Hama both describe a colour by three attributes:

  • Hue. The quality we call red, yellow, green, blue and so on, set by the part of the spectrum a colour reflects most. The purples, which mix the spectrum's two ends, red and violet, join those ends, so the hues run on with no start or end, and are often drawn as a circle.
  • Lightness. How light or dark it is, matched against a scale of greys.
  • Saturation. How strong the colour is, against the pure one. Wada's 1934 booklet treats it as depth, so red thinned with water is paler, and red laid over red deeper. Hama keeps depth apart, as a colour laid over itself takes on black and grows darker. Many colour systems today use a close relative, chroma, which measures how far a colour is from the grey of the same lightness.

Tone brings the last two together. Colours of different hues with about the same lightness and saturation, all pale, say, or all dark and greyed, share a tone. Much of the advice in Guidance is about tones.

Mixing a colour with white gives its tints, with black its shades, and with grey its greyed tones, so one hue gives hundreds of colours. Ostwald, whose system Wada used, drew them as a triangle, with the full colour at one corner and white and black at the other two. Stood round an axis of greys, the triangles of all the hues make a three-dimensional shape, two cones joined at their bases. This colour solid has white at the top, black at the bottom and the full colours round its widest part, the middle (see the Ostwald view).

Full colours aren't equally light, though. Matched against greys, yellow is the lightest, then orange-yellow and yellow-green, then orange and green, red and blue-green, blue and red-purple, and purple is the darkest.

Hama's view: in the colour solid, the higher a colour sits, the lighter it is, yet every full colour sits on the same ring round the middle, as if all were equally light. Hama noted that this simplifies things. Yellow's full colour is much lighter than blue's, so it should sit higher, nearer the white, and blue's lower, nearer the black.

Equal amounts of white don't give equal steps of lightness either. A little white lightens a dark grey far more than a light one, so the grey scale behind the system lets its white fall by a constant ratio, not in tenths.

Since then: many systems, such as Munsell's, PCCS and OKLCH, space lightness in steps that look equal (see Colour systems), so in their colour solids the full colours sit at different heights, as Hama said they should. Not all do, and the Swedish NCS, for one, keeps Ostwald's approach.

Complementary colours

Two colours are complementary when, mixed as light or spun together on a disc, they make white or grey. They come in pairs because of how a colour is made. A pigment absorbs part of white light and reflects the rest, and the part it absorbs, seen as light on its own, is the complement of its colour. Together the two make up white light again, like twins born of the same light.

The eye makes complements too. Stare for a while at a red paper on a grey ground, then take it away, and a blue-green after-image appears in its place.

On a hue circle, a colour's complement lies roughly opposite it. Ostwald built his so that it always does. To find a colour's exact complement, spin it together with another on a disc, changing how much of each shows. Complements give a neutral grey, and any other pair leaves a trace of colour.

* The discs blur as they spin, as the eye blurs a real one. A screen shows the spin frame by frame, so on its own it blends the colours only partly.

Many painting books pair colours differently, from a circle built on red, yellow and blue paint. Each of the three is paired with the mix of the other two, so red with green, yellow with purple and blue with orange. Those pairs come from mixing paint, and paint doesn't mix like light. Yellow and blue paint make green (see Mixing colours), while yellow and blue light make white. The pairs here are those of light.

A complement is a matter of hue alone, so a colour's lighter and darker tones share it. Pink and maroon have the same complement as red. Wada drew up a table of such pairs from his own palette (see his pairs).

Mixing colours

Colours can be mixed in three ways, and each gives different results.

  • Paints subtract. Each paint absorbs part of the light, and in a mix each also absorbs some of what the other reflects. As a rule of thumb, the mix's hue lies between the two on the hue circle, and it's darker than the two averaged. The further apart they are, the duller and darker the mix, until opposite colours give grey or near black. That's why painters often dull a colour with a touch of its complement rather than with black, for a softer result.

    Some pairs break the rule, because a mix depends on all the light each paint reflects, not only on how it looks. The best known is yellow and blue. Mixed as light, they make white, as complements do. Mixed as paints, they make green. Yellow paint absorbs the blue end of the spectrum and blue paint the red end, and the green in the middle, which both reflect, is all that's left.

    Printing inks subtract in the same way, each taking away one part of the light. Cyan absorbs red, magenta green and yellow blue. Printed over one another, cyan and magenta leave only blue, magenta and yellow red, and yellow and cyan green, the primaries of light. All three together absorb nearly everything, leaving a near black. Printers add black as a fourth ink, for deep blacks and sharp text.

  • Light adds. Lights shone together add up, so a mix is lighter than either. Red and green light make yellow. The further apart two lights are on the circle, the paler the mix, and opposite colours make white.
  • Spinning and dots average. On a spinning disc, or in dots too small to see apart, colours neither subtract nor add but average. The mix is as light as the two averaged, so a complementary pair gives grey, not white. Japanese textiles have long used this, in komon, kasuri and stripes. Up close they show separate colours, from afar a mixture, and in between a beauty of their own.

Each kind of mixing starts from a few colours, its primaries, chosen so that their mixes give as many colours as possible. For light they are red, green and blue. For paints, long practice settled on red, yellow and blue.

With paints, which red, yellow and blue you choose matters. The most colours come from a yellow leaning to neither orange nor green, a slightly greenish blue such as Prussian blue, and a purplish red such as carmine. Even so, no choice suits every mix. Primaries that give a clear green give dull purples, and the other way round.

Since then: the primaries for paints and inks are taken to be cyan, magenta and yellow, close to the greenish blue and purplish red above, and red, yellow and blue are seen as a painters' tradition. No three primaries, of paint or of light, mix every colour either. They give every hue, but not every hue at its strongest.

Contrast

A colour doesn't look the same everywhere. What lies beside it, and what was seen just before, changes how it looks. This is contrast.

Side by side, two colours push each other apart, in three ways that often appear together.

  • Hue. Each colour looks tinged with the other's complement, so the two seem further apart on the hue circle than they are. The closer the two hues, the bigger the shift. Complements don't shift at all, and each shows its own hue at its fullest. A grey on a coloured ground takes on a tinge of the ground's complement.

  • Lightness. A colour looks lighter beside a darker one, and darker beside a lighter one. White looks whitest on black.

  • Saturation. A pale colour beside a vivid one looks paler still.

The larger, more saturated or lighter colour pushes harder, and two equal ones push each other alike. It happens where two colours meet on one surface with nothing marking the edge. An outline, a gap or an overlap between them stops it, which may be one reason white, grey or black between colours works.

One after another, colours change too, because the after-image of the first, its complement, mixes into the next. A colour seen just after its complement looks more vivid. Seen after the same or a close hue, it looks duller, and a close hue also pushes it away, so orange seen after red looks yellower.

What changes a colour

A colour isn't fixed in the thing that carries it. The same paint looks different in another light, on another material, at another size, and as it ages.

  • The light. Daylight comes from two places, the sun and the sky. On its way through the air, sunlight loses some of its blue, which the air scatters across the sky. So direct sun is orange-yellow, the sky is blue, and together they make white. Strong sun favours reds, oranges and yellows and dulls blues and purples. Quince, pomegranate and marigold blaze at noon, while wisteria, iris and hydrangea are at their best in rain, haze and the soft light of morning and evening.

    Lamplight depends on the lamp, but it's usually richer in orange and poorer in blue than daylight. Under it, reds and oranges glow, yellow turns whitish, green leans to yellow-green, blues darken and purple turns redder, so colour is hard to judge. A coloured light goes further. Colours of its own hue look white, and its complement grey or black.

    Since then: the eye adapts to the light more than these lists suggest. After a few minutes under lamplight it discounts much of the light's colour, so the shifts show most at first, or beside daylight. Two colours that match in one light can still differ in another.

  • Dim light. At dusk, red goes dark first and yellow loses its colour, while greens and blues still show theirs. In faint light the eye's cones, which see colour, gradually stop working, and its rods take over. Rods see only light and dark, and they respond most to blue-green, so blues and greens keep their lightness, while reds go almost black.

  • The material. Part of the light bounces off a surface as white before it reaches the colour, and the more it does, the whiter the colour looks. Water fills a rough surface and cuts this down, so a colour looks deeper wet than dry. For the same reason, pastels look light, oils dark and deep, and watercolours in between.

    What the colour sits on matters too. The same dye looks different on silk, cotton or hemp, because their fibres reflect light differently. A colour thinned with water is transparent and lets the ground show through, while one thinned with white is opaque and hides it.

  • The area. Wada warned that large areas look paler and weaker than small swatches.

    Since then: measurements find that a large area does look lighter, but more vivid, not weaker, so a wall may need a paint a step greyer than its sample.

  • Time. Colours fade. Mineral pigments last, while plant dyes and coal-tar dyes change and fade far sooner, and white paper yellows in sunlight and air. Wada used only pigments that had passed a fading test.

    Since then: many synthetic pigments last as well as mineral ones, and paints now carry a lightfastness rating, pigment by pigment.

Strong and weak, warm and cool

Colours also carry feelings. Some excite more than others, some feel warm or cool, and many carry meanings.

  • Strength. Some colours excite the eye more than others, in Wada's view mostly by how strong they are, and partly by their hue. By hue, red excites most, then orange, yellow, green, blue and indigo, and violet least. People differ, but this order is generally accepted, and red catches the eye so easily that it's used for danger signals. Strong combinations look splendid at first sight but tend to vulgarity and tire quickly, so they suit advertising, shop fronts and the stage better than the home (see Using a combination).

    Since then: studies find that how strong a colour is, its saturation and lightness, matters far more than its hue, as Wada's first point says, and no fixed order by hue holds. Red for danger is partly convention.

  • Warm and cool. Red, orange and yellow are warm, and blue and green cool. Hama traced it to the flames of a fire and the clear water of a deep pool. Every hue has warmer and cooler kinds, though. For Wada, celadon () is a warm green and green bamboo () a cool one. Warm colours suggest warmth and intimacy. Cool ones, to Hama, suggest coolness, purity and quiet, where Europeans and Americans call them cold and chilly.

  • What colours stand for. Hama listed meanings that colours carry, "a kind of code". White and black stand for sanctity, purity and seriousness, red for power and passion, yellow for light and joy, green for peace and health, blue for calm and depth, and purple for grace and nobility. But which colours people like depends on custom, age, education and surroundings, he added, so no absolute law can be laid down.

    Since then: some associations are widely shared, such as red with anger and with love, but many vary by culture. In Japan, white was long the colour of mourning.

Colour systems

A colour system orders colours by their attributes, so that any colour can be found and written down. This site places Wada's colours in four, each a view under Colour systems.

  • Ostwald. Every colour is a mix of full colour, white and black, so each hue is a triangle running from its full colour to white and to black, on a circle of 100 hues. Wada held Ostwald's theory to be "far above all others" and wrote colours in it, with 16 greys from white to black, lettered A to P, which this view follows. Hama drew the triangle with amounts of white and black of his own.

    Since then: Ostwald's system has fallen out of use, and Munsell's has become the standard. The Swedish NCS still describes colours much as Ostwald did, by how much white, black and full colour they seem to hold.

  • OKLCH. A modern colour space, of lightness, chroma and hue, made so that equal steps look roughly equal. The site computes every colour in it from the book's CMYK, and uses it to show them on screen.
  • Munsell. Hue, value (lightness) and chroma, spaced to look even, with chroma reaching as far as colours go. Japan's colour standards write colours in it.
  • PCCS. Made by the Japan Color Research Institute, which grew out of the association Wada founded. Its lightness follows Munsell's value, and it adds tones (colours of like lightness and saturation across the hues), for colour harmony.

Colour names

Wada gathered colour names into books, the Shikimei Sōkan and the Shikimei-chō, published by the Japan Standard Colour Association he founded, and gave every colour of his combinations a Japanese name and an English one. The English names come from Robert Ridgway's Color Standards and Color Nomenclature, a few misspelled, such as Antwarp Blue. Plate 8 of the 1935 atlas Wada made with Hama, Hyōjun shikisai zushū, shows 12 hues, each as tint, full colour and shade, with Wada's names. All 36 are colours here, 35 matched by name and one by our own reading of the plate, and their dialogs say so.

Hama adds notes on Japanese names. Ao long meant both green and blue. The browns (cha) and the mouse greys (nezumi) were the two great families of colour names in the Edo period, and both spread far. Cha, at first the colour of coarse tea, came to take in reds and even greens, such as bush-warbler brown, and nezumi took in blues and purples, such as wisteria grey. Kyoto's purple was redder than Edo's.

Wada Sanzō 和田三造

Wada Sanzō (和田三造, 1883–1967), a Japanese painter and designer, pioneered colour standards in Japan.

He trained in Western-style painting under Kuroda Seiki from 1899, and at the Tokyo School of Fine Arts from 1901 to 1904. In 1909 the Ministry of Education sent him to study in Europe: he travelled mainly in France, studying craft design as well as painting. On his way home, in 1914, he studied Eastern art in India and Burma. In 1915 he went back to Japan.

In 1927 he founded the Japan Standard Color Association, out of a concern that Japan needed standard colours.

From 1932 to 1944 he taught design at the Tokyo School of Fine Arts, as a professor.

His book, the Haishoku Sōkan (配色總鑑), he wrote, was not scientific: it came from his sense of colour as a painter, who had learned, painting from nature, to take apart its complex tones and put them together again. He also drew on European colour work: Ostwald's system to write colours down, Chevreul's rules for harmony, Ridgway's colour names.

His work on colour

Two parts of his work on colour lead to this site: a common reference for colours, and how to combine them well.

  • Standard colours. He made Japan's first systematic colour samples, and founded the association that became the Japan Color Research Institute, which went on to make the country's standard colour charts: the first in Japan with steps that look even, and the first chips anywhere of Munsell's revised system. It also helped draft JIS Z 8721, Japan's industrial standard for writing a colour down by its hue, lightness and saturation. To tie names to colours, his association put some 600 colour names onto 171 samples, each with its Japanese, Chinese and English names and its code in Ostwald's notation.
  • Colour combinations. The Haishoku Sōkan sets out 348 combinations of two, three and four colours, painted by hand, with his advice on using them (see Guidance). It is a reference for choosing colours that work together, in clothes, interiors, crafts or advertising. It was the first of the many books of colour combinations since.

Hama Yaohiko

Hama Yaohiko (濱八百彦, 1866–1944) was a painter and teacher who made colour his study. He trained in painting under the Kyoto painter Mochizuki Gyokusen, and by 1918 he was a professor at the Gakushūin, the Peers' School in Tokyo.

Colour first puzzled him in the late 1890s, at the normal school in Kagoshima, a college for teachers. Testing new students for colour blindness, he met several who saw colours quite differently from him, and he began to ask why anyone sees red or blue at all. The books he found didn't answer it, so he studied colour in his spare time, reading foreign colour scientists and making experiments of his own. Twenty years on he published Iro no kenkyū (色の研究, A study of colour, 1918), and later Shikisai gairon (色彩概論, An outline of colour, 1928), not a summary of Western work, he wrote, but a colour science of his own.

In 1935 he and Wada made a standard colour atlas, the Hyōjun shikisai zushū, fifteen large wall charts, made to teach colour to a room. Technicians under Wada's supervision coloured most of the plates by hand, and Hama wrote the commentary, an introduction to colour from light and sight to mixing, contrast and harmony. His advice is part of Guidance, and his explanations fill in much of Colour theory.

This site

This site is built on Wada's colours and colour combinations. It shows his 171 colours and his combinations on screen, to browse, compare and use in your own work.

Wada's colours and combinations come from two books. Seigensha reprinted his Haishoku Sōkan as A Dictionary of Color Combinations, and a second volume adds his seasonal combinations.

Each colour is computed from the books' CMYK, using the Japan Color 2011 Coated profile, the standard for offset printing on coated paper in Japan.

The site also shows how Wada's palette sits in four colour systems:

  • Ostwald. Every colour as a mix of a full colour with white and black. The one Wada used in his work.
  • Munsell. Hue, value (lightness) and chroma, spaced so that the steps look even. The one Japan's colour standards use.
  • PCCS. Its lightness follows Munsell's value, and it groups colours into tones, of similar lightness and saturation across the hues, for colour harmony. Made by the institute that grew out of Wada's association.
  • OKLCH. A modern colour space of lightness, chroma and hue, made so that equal steps look about equally different.

The site has six main topics:

  • Wada colours. Wada's palette, grouped by colour family as in the book, as a grid, as rows or as cards.
  • Colour systems. Wada's colours placed in four colour systems (see Colour theory).
  • Combinations. The 348 combinations of the first volume, laid out as in the book.
  • Seasons. The 72 seasonal combinations of the second volume, month by month.
  • Create. A small tool to create your own combinations, from Wada's colours or any colour of the four systems.
  • About. This page, plus the colour theory behind combinations (Colour theory) and Wada's and Hama Yaohiko's advice on choosing colours and on making and using combinations (Guidance).
